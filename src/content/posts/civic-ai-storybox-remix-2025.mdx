*A story shared at the REMIX Summit by Dr Sarah Barns, Co-Director of STORYBOX by Studio ESEM, and researcher and public historian at the RMIT Centre for Urban Research.*

Hi, I'm Sarah Barns, a creative director at Studio ESEM and Senior Research Fellow at RMIT University.

**I'm here to tell a story today.**

**Before I do though, I'd like to pay my respects to Australia's First storytellers: here close to Warrane, the Gadigal storytellers, but also to First Nations storytellers from other lands joining us today.**

## The story begins

It's a story about the past, present and future of public space media and civic AI.

## Imagine the scene: it's 1945

**We're standing in Martin Place, Sydney.**

Australian citizens can be heard everywhere celebrating the end of a horrible war.

Australia's public broadcaster, the ABC, is here to record the action with its outside broadcast van.

Using the latest in broadcasting technology, the sounds of this space are being shared with those who can't be physically present.

It's the first time they've done this on Australian soil.

Let's listen in.

![Slide: Picture the scene, Martin Place, 1945, with crowds and the ABC outside broadcast van.](/images/remix-2025-1945-martin-place.jpg)

<AudioEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/remix-2025/vp-day-1945.mp3" title="Martin Place, 1945" caption="ABC outside broadcast from Martin Place, 1945." />

Audiences everywhere are entranced by the sounds of actually being there, in public spaces, live, even from the comforts of their living rooms.

## Fast forward now to 2020

**The bushfires have swept through NSW, and then COVID's arrived. We all got scared, suddenly very used to wearing masks in public spaces.**

A new ABC initiative has launched in Parramatta Square with some upstarts who created a digital exhibition initiative for public spaces, which they called STORYBOX.

Having watched the way the once-open internet of the 1990s had been steadily eroded by these secret data strategies of US-based technology companies, these creatives were looking to **reignite the possibilities of public space media, specifically in partnership with Australia's public broadcaster.**

They wanted to know: could public spaces be spaces for convening more inclusive, less destructive conversations, perhaps building on the original idea of the Agora?

Could public spaces be more central to the engagement strategies of cultural and arts organisations, to museums, as custodians of collections and champions of civic learning?

![Slide: Picture the scene, Parramatta Square, 2020. ABC partners with STORYBOX in Western Sydney.](/images/remix-2025-2020-parramatta.jpg)

<AudioEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/remix-2025/parramatta-launch-2020.mp3" title="STORYBOX launch, Parramatta, 2020" caption="The Mayor of Parramatta at the time of the STORYBOX launch." />

What they found when they set out in Parramatta Square astounded and excited them.

*Yes, everyday people really wanted to be a part of this STORYBOX experiment!*

People called them up on the telephone: they'd seen themselves in ABC archival media on STORYBOX as a young person, when they'd arrived from Ukraine. From Lebanon, after the war.

People wanted to share their own stories, real and imagined.

The creators of this STORYBOX program were astounded by the creativity and enthusiasm of the community expressed through STORYBOX.

They were also impressed by the reach they gained, just by being outdoors. Even in COVID times, over 400,000 people passed by.

![Slide: a wall of STORYBOX portraits and stories from communities and storytellers.](/images/remix-2025-stories.jpg)

Over the next five years, the experiment with STORYBOX in public spaces continued to evolve.

More and more people and institutions got involved. First Nations stories were foundational to the revisioning of place: from colonial narratives to First Nations-led narratives.

And then more. Pop-up exhibitions were happening more and more frequently.

Communities, storytellers and cultural institutions started to use STORYBOX for digital exhibitions, for touring programs, for forecourt exhibitions.

The future was seen through STORYBOX, underwater scenes were brought to life. You can see this STORYBOX at the front of the Museum's foyer here.

But also, more and more audiences in public spaces connected with their STORYBOXES.

The upstart creatives didn't want to take on 'start-up funding' that required extracting public value for private gain.

Crazily perhaps, they wanted to remain **true to this old-fashioned ethos of public value in public places.**

They didn't want to be funded through gambling money or rich gambling men. They didn't go for arts grants, because what would be the point?

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/remix-2025/storybox-remix-2025.mp4" poster="/images/remix-2025-video.jpg" caption="STORYBOX in public spaces, 2025." aspectRatio="16/9" />

Through extensive collaborations with cultural and impact organisations, the dream of public space media started to appear, *as if from the mists of time…*

![Slide: STORYBOX partners, including the ABC, Australian Museum, CSIRO, City of Sydney, City of Parramatta, Sydney Opera House and National Museum of Australia.](/images/remix-2025-partners.jpg)

**But something was missing here…**

They realised, after five years of hard work, that just being a space for digital exhibition didn't really capture the ways in which digital technologies were co-opting and shaping public spaces.

They noticed that increasingly, US technology companies and their business models were looking to zero in on public spaces via AI, tracking eyeballs, and even tracking location data as people moved between public and private spaces!

Even city councils like the City of Sydney were happy to do this!

They looked on, with dismay, at how business models built on the extraction of attention were being normalised everywhere.

And they realised that the world, and Australia's public, cultural institutions, faced grave danger!

Either they could adopt AI workflows as dictated by the people championing extractive capitalism, and then deepen reliance on technology overlords, or they could start to think much more proactively about what it actually meant to be a **guardian of citizenship in an age of AI.**

**They realised: public space media needed a public space data strategy.**

They realised they needed to actually work with their cultural partners to figure out a better future together. Embracing AI, but on their own terms.

**Yes. They still held faith in the idea that technology could be used for public purpose: to enliven connections, create healthier lives, and even fund art!**

While all the problems of the world, and AI, seemed really complicated, they realised a simple change in thinking could be a good place to start.

**Citizen-first data strategy!**

![Slide: STORYBOX impact model linking educators, citizens and place custodians.](/images/remix-2025-impact.jpg)

They started with a simple proposition: to treat 'users' of public spaces as citizens.

Citizens with feelings, with positive aspirations, with shared connections, with an inherent love of creativity. Not just consumers who needed to buy things to be happy.

*They saw an ex-advertising guy get lots of attention with his book Citizens, and figured they were probably onto something.*

What would it take to redesign data ecosystems in public spaces around the idea of citizenship?

How could they rethink all this data in public spaces, building shared cultural value, and respecting privacy?

They started to build speculative AI workflows in service of citizens, not consumers.

(Psst… take a [MoodRing survey](https://moodring.storybox.co/4/create) for the STORYBOX at the Australian Maritime Museum.)

![Slide: Civic AI. Enabling the value and impact of public space media through citizen-focused impact workflows.](/images/remix-2025-civic-ai.jpg)

They developed new indicators of value, designed to enhance the way cultural institutions reported their own value and impact to their funders and sponsors.

## Fast forward to 2031

![Slide: Picture the scene, Bankstown, 2031.](/images/remix-2025-2031-bankstown.jpg)

Australia's public and cultural institutions, including the ABC, have re-embraced the value of public spaces as a core platform for publicly funded media that strives to support positive citizenship.

Having learned from their mistakes of the early 2000s, they've invested in public space media ecosystems, and are now working across Australian cities and communities to support thriving digital storytelling communities that link health care technologies and applications, regenerative urban forest care, multi-generational educational campuses and thriving hubs of arts and culture.

They're promoting the revival of First Nations languages, using the principles of Indigenous data sovereignty. And they're teaching young people about soil health.

Communities of learners can tune into scenes like the one I described from 1945, creating local pop-up installations using open archives to bring the past into the present in novel and inspiring ways, reminding everyone of the power of Australia's citizens through time, working time and time again to defend the country from the reaches of fascism.

Using the very latest in AI technology, they've developed trusted algorithms built to enhance investments in local community infrastructure, generating spillover effects of some $15bn, as public spending previously allocated via clunky off-site agencies is now more closely integrated into citizens' local monitoring of ecosystems and care-based activities.

Australia has, through the digital leadership of its public and cultural agencies, become **a major hub for a new wave of civic AI advancement and experimentalism.**

A series of mirrored sandboxes have sprung up around the world, linking democratic agencies with trusted, privacy-preserving algorithms and interfaces that enable many Australian citizens to **devote significant portions of their days to supporting and enhancing local civic infrastructure during the climate emergency.**

Citizen care of people and place has now been integrated into health care indicators, relieving health agencies of **huge budgets** previously spent on people who had become sick through loneliness, atomisation and inactivity.

And public agencies are finding they have armies of resilience corps who invest in protecting communities during increasingly chaotic weather events.

This is all powered by the new **civic technology stack** that prioritises the values and benefits of **place-based interactions** between citizens and places.

Cultural and scientific institutions, museums, public broadcasters, and agencies funded to support public value outcomes have evolved to become one of the largest value-creating technology infrastructures in the world.

**Local community data hubs are now used as central platforms for public space media, which distribute data value locally in the form of local investments to provide free health care, energy, nature-care, education and, of course, funding for arts and creativity.**

## How did they get here, you may ask?

The pivot towards civic AI took hard work: lots of technology design and, of course, new protocols and standards to shift the way personal data was being extracted by large technology companies to fuel hyper-consumption by atomised individuals.

But really, all this work was made possible by a simple but powerful recognition: that for those funded by taxpayers, the work is to design technology solutions **around the needs of place-based citizens, not atomised consumers.**

It took a difficult decision by Australia's well-funded public media organisations to stop investing in extractive technology overlords from anti-democratic nations, and instead focus on building 21st-century civic technology designed to foster thriving relationships between people and places.

It also took a realisation that Australia's greatest technology innovations have happened when public institutions **invest in local companies and service providers, creating healthy and thriving technology ecosystems that support local jobs, build local capacity and skills, including AI skills.**

The little STORYBOX program created by those upstarts, way back when, played a little part in this journey.

But its creators were proud of their contribution.

They'd helped to show that public spaces, public value and citizenship matter dearly to the future of arts, creativity, human and ecological health, and indeed, what it means to be human in an era of AI.

*The end. Thank you!*

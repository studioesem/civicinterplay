*From the archive. A conversation between Indy Johar and Dr. Sarah Barns, the first in the Planetary Civics Seminar Series, supported by RMIT University.*

## Selected topics

<div class="q-note"><p>As a strategic designer, what are the ways in which you could act as an intervention in that space? Would you like to speak about any projects that you are doing to tweak the value systems for something like a tree?</p></div>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/time-of-transformation.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-time-of-transformation.jpg" caption="Time of transformation" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="16:50">

I would argue we're living in a one in a 400 year cycle of transformation, where our theory of seeing the world through silos and objects and individual discrete things is being challenged by seeing the world in relationship to. Like the chair that you see here, the chairs, they are a flow, right? They are a flow of materials to which they are not at this moment in time, to which they have existed before and they will exist after, not in the same form, but in other forms, either as most likely as waste somewhere.

But we see things through the theory of objects, yet actually what we don't see and organise things through a series of flows. And I think that's now made possible by new technology and new capabilities that technology is offering us. And so when people talk about circularity, they're fundamentally challenging the nature of how we see the world around us.

</ClipWords>

<div class="q-note"><p>How do we work in alliance with each other to progress these terms and these ways of thinking? Do we all need a kind of agreement around common language, to relabel, rebrand, rename things that have become almost hollow by their overuse?</p></div>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/humanism.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-humanism.jpg" caption="Humanism" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="20:20">

I think the real hard work is having the courage, and I think it's courage, to re-examine ourselves. How do we respect the extraordinariness of being human? And that extraordinariness is not the deficit of the planet, because I think everything around us is pretty extraordinary. But I think there's a sort of paradigm transformation of what it means to be human, and recognising our entanglements as a key part of being human.

</ClipWords>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/re-examine-ourselves.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-re-examine-ourselves.jpg" caption="Re-examine ourselves" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="21:19">

I think one of the biggest challenges that we face is to unwind and have the courage to re-examine ourselves, and that re-examination sits at many levels, recognising that it is not, you know, my brain is not my brain, it's a social brain. I stand on the shoulders of everyone else around me, and actually that is a material reality rather than actually some heroism of like, “Oh, aren't I extraordinary?”

Actually recognising our entanglement at a deep level is not just an illusion, and you can go to quantum level, you know, people like David Bohm were talking about this beautifully in the 1970s. So you can go from a quantum level all the way to the social brain, you can go through microbiomes, all of this stuff, that actually there's a fundamental re-examination of us. And I think that is the courage space right now, to be able to allow ourselves to re-examine ourselves. And I would argue that every great revolution is rooted in re-examination of what it means to be human. And I think that is the paradigm we're in the middle of.

</ClipWords>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/planetary-species.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-planetary-species.jpg" caption="Planetary species" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="23:21">

We live as a planetary relationship with each other. And we're already living in a planetary cycle. Unfortunately, most of that planetary cycle is extractive. Most of that planetary cycle is extractive and asymmetric. But the reality is we're already a planetary species. We're already there.

</ClipWords>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/coding-institutions.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-coding-institutions.jpg" caption="Coding institutions" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="25:58">

So we've created a world view, we've incorporated it, seeded it, coded it into our institutions. And the coding of our capital institutions around us creates the asymmetric extractions, externalities. A private limited company was designed to create an asymmetric landscape of effectively shielding the extraction of wealth without holding the liabilities.

So we've created a world view, we've incorporated it, seeded it, coded it into our institutions. And I think that world view that's been coded is having to be reimagined. And that's, I think, the paradigm leap we're in the middle of, but it's rooted in the courage of re-examining what it means to be human.

</ClipWords>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/democracy-and-science.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-democracy-and-science.jpg" caption="Democracy and science" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="26:35">

The science behind our policy is just archaic. We just do not understand. We haven't created the politics for this new transformation, and the politics of actually living genuinely in a quantum world, if you want to put it through that lens, living in a world of entanglement. We haven't created the politics for it. And that's the civilisation leap.

So I think there's a really deep question about actually our pathways for democracy. We've narrowed democracy through the vote. I think that is a massive problem. And I think the vote is not sufficient to describe a 21st century democracy. And I think many people now feel entrapped in their lives, and they feel diminished in their humanity. And I think there is a corollary to that, which is that our democratic systems are no longer actually advancing a deep, meaningful democracy.

</ClipWords>

<div class="q-note"><p>Our minds are being shaped by interrelationships with platform power, while at the same time those such as yourself are calling for a much more entangled sense of self with our more-than-human worlds. I see this bifurcation potentially happening. How do we grapple with those two almost conflicting trends in what it means to be human today?</p></div>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/technology.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-technology.jpg" caption="Technology" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="28:47">

I mean, when I talk about the entanglement, I would say machine, human, ecological entanglement. I wouldn't put machines outside of us. And this is a really important conversation because I think one thing that we did, and I think we've done this in the act of othering, we've othered technology as much as we've othered trees. And when you other something, you also create a landscape of violence to that thing, and you anticipate a violence from that thing to yourself. So the distancing of things, and this was a conceptual frame, if you really go back into history, the idea of perspective, the idea of classification theory, this separation allowed for both the violence and allowed for a permission of othering. Now that distancing creates a way of seeing.

So I think in a certain thesis we created this othering and thereby a fear of technology. Whereas I think there are other cultures like Japan and other places which have not othered technology in that way. They see technology as a symbiosis and an entanglement with being human. So I think there's something really interesting about maybe not even de-othering technology, but re-entangling ourselves with technology, and talking about a machine, human, ecological symbiosis that we're seeing.

And this is where people like James Lovelock say this more eloquently than I will. He talked beautifully about actually, for the first time, the planet, through its sensory networks, satellites and other things, is able to become self-aware of itself. So we are able to recognise the earth is heating up. That self-consciousness at the planetary scale is the emergent, he would argue, of a planetary consciousness. And that's an extraordinary idea, that the planet is becoming self-aware, and we are part of an intelligence system of the planet becoming self-aware. And that for the first time, the planet can not only be self-aware, it can be self-agent. So if there is a meteorite coming to hit the planet, the planet can actually do something about it, to preserve an ecological biosphere which is acting as a cooling agent to preserve life on this planet.

</ClipWords>

<div class="q-note"><p>How do you navigate those different institutional contexts, to work within them but then also to reinvent? And, as we're in an educational institution here, what are the ways that you see educational institutions responding to this?</p></div>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/politics-of-freedom.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-politics-of-freedom.jpg" caption="Politics of freedom" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="41:18">

I think we haven't been able to be critical about our democratic systems because actually we've been locked into a 200 or 250 year paradigm, which we are going to have to reinvent, move ourselves outside industrial democracy into a new theory of agency, a new theory of freedom as well. Language like freedom has largely never been adopted by progressives because we've always seen it through the lens of collectivism and solidarity, but actually a freedom to care, not a freedom to escape, but a freedom to be able to be human, to thereby live in care. So how do we build a new language and taxonomy around that?

</ClipWords>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/organisational-complexity.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-organisational-complexity.jpg" caption="Organisational complexity" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="42:15">

If we're operating in complexity, which we are, there are two paradigms in complexity that are really important. And one is to operate in care. It's a language that's often not used because our commercial thesis is always about transactional optimisation, optimising my position versus optimising your position. Whereas to be in a relationship of care is very critical in complexity, because you do not know the second and third order implications. So you have to live through a care that requires a different attention. And the second thing is you have to operate in a relationship of learning.

So I would say that the paradigm leap for organising is that our management theory is entirely built on a theory of control, and the control from the C-suite downwards. And I think in a complex world, you have to replace the CEO with the chief learning officer. And agency needs to be at the front line, and the role of the executive is to actually scaffold the learning of the system. And coherence of a system comes through compounding learning, not through centralised control. And this is not a moral crusade from my perspective. This is just how do you deal with complexity? So if we acknowledge we're living in a complex, entangled world, how do you build organisation to operate in complexity through a relationship of care and a relationship of compounding learning?

</ClipWords>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/new-organisations.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-new-organisations.jpg" caption="New organisations" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="44:31">

In moments like this, so one in a 400 year paradigm leap of how we relate to the world, I think we go through a moment where siloed knowledge bases have less power than polymathic knowledge bases, or entangled or intersectional knowledge bases. And that I think is a real challenge, because our universities and our learning institutions and many of our organisations are designed around siloed operations. It's very difficult, we were talking about this earlier today, our institutional logics, our institutional incentives are all geared towards these siloed knowledge bases.

It's been going down in pretty much all the universities. And that is because our institutional logics no longer function to actually build the polymathic disciplines, the disciplines at the intersection of disciplines. And that I think is going to be the great paradigm leap. And I think that when you compound that with machine-assisted learning, compound learning capabilities, machines which ask humans questions to direct better discovery, I think we open up a whole new paradigm of learning in that way.

</ClipWords>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/selects/learning-institutions.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-learning-institutions.jpg" caption="Learning institutions" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="46:08">

If you imagine the orchestration of society through a compound learning framework, I think we create whole new economies of learning, which are beyond and entangled with the institution. So it's not that I go to the institution for four or five years of my life, but actually these are new knowledge institutions that are co-living with us in a completely different way.

So I think we're living in a paradigm leap, which is actually in the process of going from the big macro public private divisionality to a systems entangled view, then all the way through to all the paradigms of institutions. And this manifests itself in the design, legal design of institutions, and the coding of everything around us, from employment contracts to these illusions of consultancy contracts, commodity ideas. Most of our economy is constructed through that theory of commodity or pre-prescribed value, which doesn't work with complexity.

</ClipWords>

<div class="q-note"><p>From the floor: I know that you can chip away in little bits, but how can you make enough change? How can we make big differences quite quickly?</p></div>

<VideoEmbed src="https://media.civicinterplay.io/posts/indy-johar-2023/rate-of-change.mp4" poster="/images/indy-johar-2023-rate-of-change.jpg" caption="Rate of change" aspectRatio="16/9" />

<ClipWords time="1:04:44">

And what I'm finding interesting right now is that a year ago, a year and a half ago, a lot of what I was saying was like, I'd get invited to boardrooms, you know, and it was like, “Oh, that's lovely, Indy. Great lunch speech. Now we can get back to business.” And what I'm finding interesting is the same CEOs and leaders are phoning me up and saying, yeah, we don't think it works. And I had a major bank phone us up and say, yeah, we've been doing ESG for a while and we just don't think it's going to get us home. We don't think it's in any way commensurate to the scale of problem we're facing.

And that recognition is starting to happen. And that to me gives me great hope, because it starts to say that what is marginal is suddenly becoming recognised as near critical. So I would say there is a much larger space of operation than I think I was imagining even a year ago. And I think all the way from monetary production, everything is going to be on the table. Literally everything is going to be on the table. The biggest challenge for democracies right now is we cannot orchestrate for the scale of transition at the speed that's available. I was telling one major foundation recently, look, you've got to be allocating to reimagine democracy, right? Do not imagine the democracies that we have are going to be the democracies that are going to take us through.

</ClipWords>
